Decision record

Contract Term Model

Seven decisions that replace what the deal card is a card of. Settled before any more of it gets drawn.

Supersedes the Contracts & deals UI uplift Status decided, not specified Scope data model · sync · surface

Why this replaced a redesign

Three visual passes over the deal card all came back crowded. They were going to: each one rearranged a form whose contents were fixed by a structure nobody had questioned. The card was crowded because a HubSpot deal record is not the thing a CSM is looking at, and no amount of hierarchy, grouping or detection fixes a card that is a card of the wrong object.

What follows are decisions, not a specification. They fix what exists and how it relates; they do not yet say what any screen looks like.

The object

Three levels, in combination — each owning facts the others cannot hold. That mutual exclusivity is what makes this a hierarchy rather than three views of one row.

Relationshipper account
The commercial position: what they pay now, what changed, what's coming. Where a CSM answers “how is this account doing, commercially?”
TermSignal owns
A contract period — start, end, notice deadline, what was bought, what we owe them. A renewal creates the next term rather than a sibling row.
DealHubSpot owns
The CRM record. Evidence that a term was signed or changed — not the unit of work. Read-only in Signal.

The seven decisions

  1. 01

    The unit is Relationship → Term → Deal, in combination

    Not the HubSpot deal. A term is what a contract actually is; a deal is how the CRM recorded it; a relationship is what the account is worth over time.

    Settles Succession (terms follow each other, deals attach to terms) and taxonomy — “expansion” stops being a pipeline tab and becomes what it is: a deal that raised the current term's value.

  2. 02

    Signal owns Terms. HubSpot owns Deals, read-only.

    Terms become first-class Signal records with their own columns. Deals keep syncing and stop being editable. Nothing is dual-owned.

    Settles The override model. client.properties.__deal_overrides retires, and with it the eleven “edited” badges — they existed only to explain which of two competing owners won a field. A full re-sync stops destroying CSM work, which it does today.

    Unlocks Notice period, obligations and true contract dates — none of which HubSpot has anywhere to put.

  3. 03

    The system proposes a Term; a CSM confirms it

    A closed-won deal produces a proposed term with dates and value pre-filled. Notice period and obligations start empty — those are exactly the facts only a human has, and they are what confirmation is for.

    Settles Nothing is asserted as verified until someone agrees, and nobody faces a blank page. “Terms awaiting confirmation” becomes a real work queue that falls out of the model rather than being bolted on.

  4. 04

    Unconfirmed terms count, carrying a visible unconfirmed state

    ARR, account status and the 120-day renewal window keep working from day one. Confirmation raises confidence; it does not switch numbers on.

    Costs

    The unconfirmed marker travels with the number, not the term — so it has to appear on the client header, in Reports, on Today and in portfolio ARR. If it only renders on the deal card, the model quietly lies everywhere else. This is wider than it sounds and should be scoped as its own piece.

  5. 05

    Obligations are first-class on the Term

    What we owe, not only what they bought. ARMS turns on 20 contracted custom content items and Annex 2's 12-month activation cadence with delay penalties behind it — facts that today exist only as prose in a brief nobody can query.

    Settles “Are we delivering?” becomes answerable instead of narrative, and the delay-penalty exposure becomes visible before it lands rather than after.

  6. 06

    Confirming a Term generates a project

    Obligations become project milestones from a template at confirmation. One task system, no duplicate entry, and the existing computeProjectDeadlines() and templates do the delivery work — no new definition of “late” in a codebase that already has too many.

    Costs, and the mitigation

    If the project is the only home for a commitment, reshaping or deleting a milestone silently edits what you owe. Mitigate with provenance: a contractual flag set at generation. The term can then assert “20 of 20 contractual milestones present” and show a discrepancy if one goes missing; deleting one asks for a reason rather than happening quietly.

  7. 07

    A Contracts tab, with each Term on its own route

    The tab lists the relationship and its terms; opening one goes to its own URL.

    Settles The mechanical cause of the crowding. Three levels, obligations, a generated project and a deal history were being flattened into one accordion strip inside the General information form. Addressability also matters once obligations are worked over months — a term can be linked from Today, from a notification, or shared.

Problems that stop existing

Not fixed — dissolved. These were symptoms of the old structure and have no expression in the new one.

Eleven “edited” badges competing with the content Nothing is dual-owned, so there is nothing to disambiguate
A full re-sync wipes CSM overrides Term-owned facts are never in HubSpot's path
Renewal guessed as contract start + 1 year, ignoring the term length beside it A term stores its own end date. Nothing is inferred, so the formula question disappears
A checkbox that moves ARR, needing its own save model ARR is a property of the current term, not a sum of ticked rows
The “Renewal” tab that was really “renewal or unclassified” Pipeline tabs go; a deal's place in the hierarchy replaces its CRM category
Twenty-five fields with no order of importance Three levels, each answering a different question

Still worth doing, unchanged

These were real defects in the current card and survive the model change, because they are about permissions and feedback rather than structure. They can ship before any of the above.

  • Read-only users get interactive edit controls. canEditClient never reaches GeneralTab, and OverrideField has no read-only path. The server gate is correct; the UI promises an edit it can't deliver.
  • Failed saves say nothing. Four catch { revert } blocks. A 403 and a dropped connection look identical to a value that wouldn't stick.
  • Editing an amount strips its formatting — the input shows bare 24480.
  • Extract the code. ClientProfileTabs.tsx is 3,308 lines. Move deals to components/clients/deals/ as a no-behaviour-change commit before any of this starts.

Before anything is built

This is a material product change

It alters the data model, a business rule, a calculation and a surface. Per the repository's own convention it goes through the product-manager step for a real specification — states, permissions, edge cases and acceptance criteria — before implementation. This record decides direction; it is not that specification.

Real contract history for ARMS. Only the first deal in the prototype is your data — the Go1 renewal and Perform pilot figures were invented as shape. The hierarchy can't be drawn against reality without the actual terms.
What an obligation records. What, how many, by when, done — and whether quantity and penalty exposure are modelled or left to the linked project.
Which template a confirmed term generates from, and what happens when no template fits.
Concurrent vs sequential terms. An account can hold more than one live contract at once; “the current term” needs defining when there are two.
Migration. 132 accounts, every term proposed on day one, and the unconfirmed marker reaching Reports, Today and the client header.
